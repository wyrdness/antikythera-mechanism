## Overview

The Antikythera Mechanism is a hand-powered geared device of Hellenistic Greek manufacture, dated to the 2nd or early 1st century BCE, recovered in 1900–1901 from the wreck of a cargo ship off the island of Antikythera in the southern Aegean. The mechanism survives in 82 corroded bronze fragments and is held by the National Archaeological Museum of Athens. It originally consisted of a wooden box approximately 34 × 18 × 9 cm housing at least 30 interlocking bronze gears with hand-cut triangular teeth. Operated by a hand crank, the device modelled the motions of the Sun and Moon, predicted lunar and solar eclipses through the Saros cycle, tracked the 19-year Metonic cycle, displayed the four-year cycle of the Panhellenic games on a dedicated dial, and is now believed to have indicated the positions of the five planets known to antiquity. Modern study began with Derek J. de Solla Price's pioneering 1959 and 1974 work and has been transformed since 2005 by the Antikythera Mechanism Research Project, which used high-resolution X-ray computed tomography (Bladed Surface Imaging at X-Tek) to read the inscriptions hidden in the corroded fragments. The mechanism stands as the earliest known geared analog computer and the most sophisticated surviving piece of Hellenistic mechanical technology.

## Etymology

- **Origin**: Named after the Greek island of Antikythera (Αντικύθηρα), off whose coast the wreck was discovered.
- **Meaning**: 'Antikythera' means 'opposite Kythera', i.e. the island opposite Kythera (Cythera).
- **First use**: The artifact was first identified as something more than ordinary corroded bronze on 17 May 1902, when archaeologist Valerios Stais noticed a gear embedded in one of the fragments.

## Theories & Explanations

### Scientific

**Hellenistic geared astronomical instrument** _(Derek J. de Solla Price, Tony Freeth, Mike Edmunds, Alexander Jones)_

The mainstream consensus: the mechanism is a 2nd-century BCE Hellenistic Greek astronomical computer, the product of an unbroken tradition of geared mechanical astronomy that has otherwise left only literary traces (Cicero on Archimedes' sphere).

### Skeptical

**Rejection of 'lost civilisation' framings** _(History of science community)_

Mainstream historians of science reject popular framings of the mechanism as evidence of a 'lost civilisation' or extraterrestrial intervention; the device is fully explicable within Hellenistic Greek mathematical astronomy.
